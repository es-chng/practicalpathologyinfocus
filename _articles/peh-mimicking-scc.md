article_type: Diagnostic Pitfall

pitfall: >-
  Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia, a reactive downgrowth of squamous
  epithelium, is mistaken for invasive well-differentiated squamous cell
  carcinoma, most often in small, superficial or tangentially cut biopsies.

why_it_matters: |-
  Overdiagnosis can lead to unnecessary wide excision or radical surgery for a
  benign, reactive process. Just as importantly, calling the lesion carcinoma
  can hide the real diagnosis underneath it: a granular cell tumour, a deep
  fungal or mycobacterial infection, or a chronic ulcer that needs treatment
  of its own.

  The reverse error also occurs. A well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma
  can be dismissed as reactive when the biopsy is superficial and atypia is
  subtle, so the aim is not to favour either diagnosis but to know what each
  requires.

when_it_happens: |-
  The pitfall is most likely when the biopsy is small, fragmented or
  tangentially sectioned, so that islands of epithelium appear detached from
  the surface. Common settings include:

  - Overlying a granular cell tumour, in skin, oral mucosa or larynx
  - Deep fungal and mycobacterial infections
  - The edges of chronic ulcers, burns and scars
  - Some cutaneous lymphoid infiltrates, including CD30-positive
    lymphoproliferative disorders

comparison:
- feature: Cytology
  favours_mimic: Bland, maturing keratinocytes; little nuclear atypia
  favours_diagnosis: Nuclear pleomorphism and atypia, which may be subtle in well-differentiated tumours
- feature: Mitoses
  favours_mimic: Few, typical, confined to the basal layers
  favours_diagnosis: Atypical forms, or mitoses well above the basal layer
- feature: Architecture
  favours_mimic: Irregular tongues and strands that remain connected to the surface
  favours_diagnosis: Infiltrating nests, cords and single cells, often extending deeper
- feature: Stroma
  favours_mimic: Inflamed, often with granulation tissue or the underlying cause
  favours_diagnosis: Desmoplastic reaction around invasive nests
- feature: Underlying process
  favours_mimic: Frequently present, and explains the hyperplasia
  favours_diagnosis: None required

key_clue: >-
  Look beneath the epithelium. A granular cell tumour, organisms, abscesses or
  an ulcer bed often explains the hyperplasia; once the cause is found, the
  apparent carcinoma usually loses its significance.

ancillary_tests: |-
  Special stains look for the cause rather than separating the two epithelial
  processes: periodic acid–Schiff or Grocott methenamine silver for fungi,
  acid-fast stains for mycobacteria, and S100 or SOX10 for the cells of a
  granular cell tumour. Proliferation and p53 immunostains do not reliably
  distinguish reactive from neoplastic epithelium here and should not settle
  the question on their own.

how_to_avoid:
- Do not diagnose invasive carcinoma on a superficial or tangential biopsy without convincing cytological atypia.
- Search the stroma for an underlying cause, and request special stains when the setting suggests one.
- Correlate with the clinical picture, including any history of ulceration, infection or a mass.
- When in doubt, report descriptively and recommend a deeper or repeat biopsy rather than forcing a diagnosis.

bottom_line: >-
  Pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia is reactive. In a small biopsy, irregular
  squamous downgrowth without convincing atypia should prompt a search for its
  cause, such as a granular cell tumour, infection or ulcer, before a diagnosis
  of carcinoma is made.
