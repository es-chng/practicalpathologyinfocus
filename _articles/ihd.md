question: |-
  How does myocardial ischemia progress from coronary plaque to myocardial infarction and scar, and how do morphologic features guide the dating of infarction, recognition of complications, and clinical management?
background: |-
  Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. It is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for approximately nine million deaths each year, although age-adjusted mortality in high-income countries has fallen by more than half since the 1960s through prevention, better therapy, and lifestyle change. The principal cause is obstructive atherosclerosis of the epicardial coronary arteries. Stable angina and acute coronary syndromes are not points on a single scale of stenosis severity: they are distinct clinical entities with different plaque biology, presentation, and management. This educational review follows the disease from etiology and plaque biology through the ischemic myocardium, the morphologic evolution of infarction, reperfusion in the acute and chronic settings, initial management, complications, and synthesis.
sections:
- heading: Foundations
  question: What is myocardial ischemia, and what is the spectrum of clinical syndromes it produces?
  content: |-
    Myocardial ischemia is inadequate perfusion relative to metabolic demand. It differs from pure hypoxemia because substrate delivery and removal of metabolites are also impaired, so ischemic injury evolves faster than hypoxic injury alone. The principal cause is obstructive atherosclerosis of the epicardial coronary arteries, which is why ischemic heart disease is often used synonymously with coronary artery disease. Lesions favour the proximal segments and branch points of the left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right coronary (RCA) arteries.

    **Mechanisms of Diminished Coronary Supply**
    - **Vascular blockages:** Driven primarily by atherosclerosis (accounting for >90% of instances) via chronic stenosis or acute thrombotic events.
    - **Epicardial coronary spasm:** Transient vessel constriction, as in Prinzmetal (variant) angina or with stimulant drugs such as cocaine.
    - **Embolic obstructions:** Clots migrating from atrial fibrillation, ventricular thrombi, or infected heart valves.
    - **Inflammation and structural tears:** Conditions impacting the vessel wall, such as vasculitis or spontaneous arterial dissections.
    - **Small vessel damage:** Microvascular dysfunction typically stemming from long-term diabetes or hypertension.
    - **Systemic delivery failures:** Reduced systemic perfusion or low blood oxygen levels caused by shock and anemia.

    **Acute Triggers Affecting the Supply-Demand Equilibrium**
    - **Rapid heart rate (tachycardia):** Increases oxygen requirements while simultaneously restricting supply by shortening diastolic filling time, the phase of the cardiac cycle during which coronary perfusion occurs.
    - **Enlarged heart muscle (hypertrophy):** Amplifies oxygen demand due to larger tissue mass, while relative capillary density falls.
    - **Low oxygen delivery (anemia/hypoxemia):** Leaves cardiac demand unchanged but severely reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
    - **Low blood pressure (hypotension):** Reduces coronary perfusion pressure, the driving force for flow through the coronary arteries.

    **Clinical Spectrum of Ischemic Heart Disease**
    - **Angina pectoris (stable, unstable, Prinzmetal):** Occurs due to temporary ischemia without causing cell death. It presents as reversible chest pain.
    - **Myocardial infarction (MI):** Results from extended ischemia that leads to permanent heart muscle necrosis. It presents as an acute heart attack that eventually heals via scar tissue formation.
    - **Chronic ischemic heart disease:** Driven by a gradual loss of heart cells, ongoing scarring, and structural remodeling. It ultimately progresses to ischemic cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure.
    - **Sudden cardiac death (SCD):** Triggered by a fatal arrhythmia arising from a sudden lack of blood flow or old scar tissue. It results in death occurring within roughly an hour of symptom onset.

    **Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)**
    - A collective clinical classification that encompasses unstable angina, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

- heading: Pathogenesis
  question: |-
    What distinguishes a stable atherosclerotic plaque from a vulnerable plaque, and how does acute plaque change produce an acute coronary syndrome?
  content: |-
    There are two separate routes to myocardial ischemia, and they should not be conflated.

    | Feature | Chronic fixed stenosis | Acute plaque change |
    |---|---|---|
    | Plaque | Stable: thick fibrous cap, small lipid core, little inflammation | Vulnerable: thin fibrous cap, large necrotic core, heavy inflammation |
    | Mechanism | Gradual luminal narrowing | Rupture, fissure, ulceration, or erosion with thrombosis |
    | Degree of stenosis | A diameter reduction of ≥70% is conventionally considered anatomically significant for non-left-main disease, but angiographic severity does not reliably predict physiological ischemia; intermediate lesions require physiological assessment when revascularization is being considered | Often only moderate (<70%) before the event |
    | Collaterals | Have time to develop and limit ischemia | Little time to develop |
    | Clinical result | Stable angina; chronic ischemic heart disease | Unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI, sudden death |

    Note that a 75% loss of cross-sectional area corresponds to only a 50% reduction in luminal diameter; these geometric thresholds describe coronary anatomy, not physiological significance. A stable plaque may remain quiescent for years and does not become an acute coronary syndrome simply by enlarging. Stenosis severity poorly predicts which plaque will rupture, and the lesion that causes angina and the lesion that later ruptures are frequently different plaques in the same patient.

    Persistent coronary occlusion increases the likelihood of a large transmural infarction and ST-elevation presentation, whereas transient or incomplete occlusion more often produces subendocardial infarction or unstable angina. The relationship is not absolute: collateral flow, the duration of occlusion, and spontaneous or therapeutic reperfusion modify both infarct extent and ECG presentation, and NSTEMI can occur with severe or complete occlusion. Statins stabilize plaques by reducing inflammation and improving endothelial function, a benefit that extends beyond lowering LDL cholesterol.

    ## Plaque rupture and plaque erosion
    Coronary thrombosis primarily occurs through two distinct pathological mechanisms: plaque rupture and plaque erosion. These are distinct substrates, and not every culprit plaque fits the classical thin-cap fibroatheroma model.

    * Plaque rupture is responsible for most coronary thrombotic events, characteristically presenting in older men with traditional cardiovascular risk factors. Pathologically, it is defined by a broken, critically thin fibrous cap (under 65 µm) that overlies a massive necrotic lipid core heavily infiltrated by macrophages; this exposure triggers a fibrin-dominant "red" clot rich in erythrocytes.
    * Plaque erosion accounts for roughly one-quarter to one-third of coronary clots and is more frequently seen in younger patients, women, and smokers, often manifesting as non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes. Unlike rupture, an eroded plaque features a structurally intact fibrous cap that has simply lost its endothelial lining. Its underlying structure contains little to no lipid core, consisting instead of smooth muscle cells and proteoglycans, and it provokes a platelet-dominant "white" thrombus.

    At autopsy, cutting the culprit segment at 3 to 5 mm intervals and taking step sections through the base of the thrombus helps separate rupture from erosion.

    ## The Susceptible Cardiovascular Patient
    Patient risk encompasses more than just the structural integrity of an isolated arterial blockage. Inflammation contributes importantly across plaque progression, thrombosis, and myocardial vulnerability, but coronary risk is multifactorial: age, cumulative LDL exposure, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, renal disease, hemodynamic factors, and thrombogenicity all contribute. This helps explain why individuals with similar angiographic blockages can follow very different clinical courses.

    This overall patient vulnerability is dictated by three interconnected areas:
    * The High-Risk Plaque: Driven by active localized inflammation alongside the total burden, severity, and specific anatomical site of the arterial disease.
    * Pro-Thrombotic Blood Conditions: Governed by systemic inflammatory pathways, tobacco use, inherited genetic predispositions, and metabolic or systemic comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic kidney disease.
    * Myocardial Vulnerability: Determined by the overall timeframe and severity of the ischemic event, individual genetic or physiological susceptibility, and concurrent microvascular damage.

    ## Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction
    Myocardial injury is defined as a cardiac troponin (cTn) value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit. The injury is classified as acute if there is a distinct dynamic rise and/or fall in troponin values (stable, flat elevations point instead toward a chronic myocardial injury).

    An acute myocardial infarction is diagnosed when acute myocardial injury coexists with clinical evidence of ischemia, as indicated by at least one of the following criteria:
    * Clinical symptoms of myocardial ischemia.
    * New ischemic electrocardiographic (ECG) changes.
    * Development of new pathological Q waves.
    * Imaging evidence demonstrating a new loss of viable myocardium or a new regional wall-motion abnormality.
    * Detection of a coronary thrombus via angiography or at autopsy.

    | Type | Mechanism | Typical setting |
    |---|---|---|
    | Type 1 | Atherothrombotic plaque disruption with thrombus | Classic spontaneous ACS |
    | Type 2 | Acute myocardial injury from oxygen supply–demand imbalance unrelated to acute coronary atherothrombosis | Sepsis, anemia, tachyarrhythmia, hypotension, spasm, SCAD |
    | Type 3 | Cardiac death with suggestive symptoms before biomarkers are available | Sudden death; often confirmed at autopsy |
    | Type 4a | Related to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) | Procedural myocardial infarction |
    | Type 4b | Related to stent thrombosis | Confirmed by angiography or autopsy |
    | Type 4c | Related to restenosis | Focal or diffuse restenosis at the prior intervention site |
    | Type 5 | Related to coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) | Associated with the surgical revascularization procedure |

    ## Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA)
    MINOCA is a working diagnosis. Alternative diagnoses that mimic MI, particularly myocarditis and Takotsubo syndrome, should be actively excluded, usually with cardiac magnetic resonance imaging where appropriate, before a definitive underlying cause is assigned.

    | Category | Examples |
    |---|---|
    | Fixed plaque unmasked by imbalance | Stable plaque with a superimposed supply-demand mismatch |
    | Non-atherosclerotic coronary disease | Spasm, small-vessel disease, vasculitis, dissection, embolism, congenital anomalies, fibromuscular dysplasia, iatrogenic causes, PCI-related no-reflow |
    | Non-coronary cardiac imbalance | Sustained tachyarrhythmia or bradyarrhythmia; left ventricular hypertrophy or dilatation |
    | Extracardiac imbalance | Respiratory failure, severe anemia, hypovolemic or septic shock |
    | Other | Sickle cell disease; amyloidosis involving intramural vessels; catecholamine excess (cocaine, pheochromocytoma) |

    At autopsy, sample intramural vessels widely and consider elastic stains for dissection and vasculitis and Congo red for amyloid.

    ## The thrombotic cascade and its drug targets

    | Step | Event | Drug target |
    |---|---|---|
    | 1. Plaque disruption | Collagen and necrotic core exposed | Statins (plaque stabilization) |
    | 2. Platelet activation | Adhesion and aggregation; release of thromboxane A2, ADP, serotonin | Aspirin (thromboxane A2); P2Y12 inhibitors (ADP); glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (final common pathway) |
    | 3. Coagulation | Tissue factor activates the cascade, generating thrombin and fibrin | Anticoagulants |
    | 4. Occlusion | Thrombus occludes the lumen within minutes | Fibrinolysis; PCI |
    | 5. Necrosis | Irreversible injury may begin after roughly 20 to 30 minutes of severe, sustained ischemia; the threshold is variable | Early reperfusion |

    Thrombus is found in about 90% of transmural infarcts examined early.

- heading: Ischemic myocardium
  question: |-
    Once coronary flow is interrupted, how quickly does the myocardium become irreversibly injured, and in what pattern does necrosis spread?
  content: |-
    **Myocardial response to ischemia**

    | Time after onset | Event | Reversible? |
    |---|---|---|
    | Seconds | Switch to anaerobic glycolysis; ATP falls, lactate rises | Yes |
    | < 1 minute | Loss of contractility | Yes |
    | Minutes | Myofibril relaxation, glycogen loss, cell and mitochondrial swelling | Yes |
    | Roughly 20 to 30 minutes | Irreversible injury may begin; the threshold varies with the severity of ischemia, collateral flow, myocardial demand, and temperature | No |
    | Several hours (often ~3 to 6) | With persistent occlusion, necrosis typically extends through the area at risk; the rate and extent depend on collateral flow, duration, and reperfusion | No |

    The earliest necrosis occurs in the subendocardium, which is perfused last, bears the highest wall stress, and has the fewest collaterals. Necrosis then advances as a wavefront from subendocardium toward epicardium over the following hours. The rate and extent of this wavefront depend on the severity and duration of ischemia, collateral flow, and reperfusion, so human infarction has no fixed clock. Early reperfusion salvages the still-viable outer wall, which is the basis of door-to-balloon targets.

    **Patterns of infarction**

    | Pattern | Mechanism | Distribution | Common causes |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Transmural | Complete, persistent occlusion of an epicardial artery | Full wall thickness within one coronary territory | Plaque rupture with occlusive thrombus |
    | Subendocardial (non-transmural), regional | Partial or transient occlusion | Inner one-third to one-half of the wall in one territory | Lysis of an occlusive thrombus; early reperfusion |
    | Subendocardial, circumferential | Global hypoperfusion | Inner wall around the whole ventricle, crossing territories | Prolonged shock or hypotension |
    | Multifocal micro-infarction | Disease of small intramural vessels | Microscopic, scattered | Microemboli, vasculitis, spasm, catecholamine toxicity |

    A circumferential subendocardial infarct that is not confined to one territory points to global hypoperfusion rather than a single culprit artery. Catecholamine excess, as with cocaine, can cause sudden death or, with repeated insults, chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy.

    **Coronary territories (right-dominant heart)**

    | Artery | Territory | ECG leads |
    |---|---|---|
    | LAD | Anterior left ventricular wall near the apex, anterior two-thirds of the septum, apex circumferentially | V1–V4 (anterior, anteroseptal, apical) |
    | RCA | Right ventricular free wall, inferior and posterior left ventricle, posterior one-third of the septum | II, III, aVF; V4R if right ventricle involved |
    | LCX | Lateral left ventricular wall except the apex | I, aVL, V5–V6; inferoposterior if left-dominant |
    | Left main, diagonal, and obtuse marginal branches | Variable and often extensive | Left main occlusion is extensive and high-risk |
    | Isolated right ventricle | Right ventricular free wall | V4R |
    | Atrium | Usually with RCA or LCX occlusion | Atrial arrhythmias |

    Inferior STEMI with hypotension and clear lungs suggests right ventricular infarction. Record right-sided leads, avoid nitrates, and give fluids cautiously.

  bottom_line: |-
    Necrosis begins in the vulnerable subendocardium after a variable interval of severe ischemia and advances as a wavefront toward the epicardium; timing determines salvage, and the pattern of infarction reflects the underlying mechanism.
- heading: Morphologic evolution
  question: |-
    Can an infarct be dated reliably from its gross and microscopic appearance, and what are the pitfalls in dating it at autopsy?
  content: |-
    Infarct morphology evolves in a predictable sequence that allows dating. The timings below apply to infarcts that have not been reperfused; reperfused infarcts evolve faster and look different (see Reperfusion). These windows are approximate: histologic dating is inherently uncertain, especially in forensic and autopsy material, and should be read as ranges rather than fixed points.

    **Gross and microscopic evolution**

    | Age | Gross | Light microscopy |
    |---|---|---|
    | 0 to ½ hour | None | None; electron microscopy shows reversible injury |
    | ½ to 4 hours | None (tetrazolium stains may detect infarcts from approximately 3 hours old) | Usually none; wavy fibres at the border |
    | 4 to 12 hours | Dark mottling begins (occasionally) | Early coagulative necrosis: hypereosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nuclei; edema; focal hemorrhage; neutrophils appear toward the end of this window |
    | 12 to 24 hours | Dark mottling | Coagulative necrosis with hypereosinophilic cytoplasm and lost nuclei; interstitial neutrophils |
    | 1 to 3 days | Mottling with a yellow-tan centre | Loss of nuclei and cross-striations; brisk neutrophilic infiltrate |
    | 3 to 7 days | Hyperemic border; central yellow-tan softening | Disintegration of dead myofibres; dying neutrophils; macrophages at the border |
    | 7 to 10 days | Maximally yellow-tan and soft; depressed red-tan margins | Well-developed phagocytosis; early granulation tissue at the margins |
    | 10 to 14 days | Red-gray depressed borders | Established granulation tissue with new vessels and collagen |
    | 2 to 8 weeks | Gray-white scar progressing from border to core | Increasing collagen, decreasing cellularity |
    | > 2 months | Dense, firm gray-white scar; wall may be thinned | Dense collagen with few fibroblasts and vessels |

    Healing begins at the margins and progresses toward the centre, so different regions of a large infarct can show different stages; date the infarct by integrating the most advanced representative changes with the overall morphology rather than relying on a single focus. Cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated and do not regenerate, and once healed a scar cannot be dated precisely.

    **Histologic dating parameters (H&E)**

    | Feature | Earliest | Full development | Decrease or disappearance |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Stretched or wavy fibres | 1–2 hours | — | — |
    | Hypereosinophilia (coagulative necrosis) | 1–3 hours | 1–3 days, with loss of striations | Disintegration after 3 days |
    | Interstitial edema | 4–12 hours | — | — |
    | Nuclear changes | 12–24 hours (pyknosis, karyorrhexis) | 1–3 days (loss of nuclei) | Depends on infarct size |
    | Neutrophil infiltration | 12–24 hours | 1–3 days | 5–7 days |
    | Neutrophil karyorrhexis | 1.5–2 days | 3–5 days | — |
    | Macrophages and lymphocytes | 3–5 days | 5–10 days, including siderophages | 10 days to 2 months |
    | Vessel and endothelial sprouts* | 5–10 days | 10 days to 4 weeks | After 4 weeks; some large dilated vessels persist |
    | Fibroblasts and young collagen* | 5–10 days | 2–4 weeks | After 4 weeks, depending on infarct size |
    | Dense fibrosis | 4 weeks | 2–3 months | Permanent |

    *Often summarized together as granulation tissue, which is maximal at 2 to 3 weeks. Adapted from Michaud et al., 2020.

    **Interpretive pitfalls**

    | Finding | Caution |
    |---|---|
    | Wavy fibres | Non-specific; also seen with agonal stretching and artefact. Never diagnose infarction on waviness alone. |
    | Hypereosinophilia | Weak evidence on its own; judge against adjacent myocardium on the same slide. |
    | Contraction band necrosis | Also caused by reperfusion, resuscitation or defibrillation, catecholamines, and handling artefact. |
    | Autolysis | Pale, fragmented myocytes; do not over-interpret. |
    | Death within about 4–6 hours | Morphology may be absent; rely on tetrazolium stains, the coronary lesion, and the history. |
    | Ancillary immunohistochemistry (C5b-9, C4d, fibronectin) | Can highlight early injury but is not fully validated. |

    A new friction rub on day two or three after a transmural infarct suggests early post-infarction pericarditis rather than Dressler syndrome.

    **Tetrazolium stains**

    | Aspect | Detail |
    |---|---|
    | Principle | Dehydrogenases and cofactors in viable myocardium reduce tetrazolium salts to a coloured formazan: red with triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC), blue-purple with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT). Infarcted muscle has lost these enzymes and stays pale. |
    | Window | Generally from approximately 3 hours after onset, before changes are visible grossly or on routine H&E; sensitivity and specificity are affected by post-mortem interval, resuscitation, sepsis, and technical factors |
    | Technique | Fresh, unfixed transverse slices about 1 cm thick, incubated in buffered stain at 37 °C and photographed promptly |
    | Limitations | False positives with a long post-mortem interval (autolysis); fixation abolishes staining; reperfused infarcts stain patchily |
    | Practice | Stain before formalin fixation; sample the pale zone and its border for histology |

    **Autopsy approach to suspected myocardial infarction**

    | Step | Practice |
    |---|---|
    | Coronary arteries | Cut transversely at 3–5 mm intervals along the main epicardial arteries; decalcify heavily calcified segments rather than forcing the blade |
    | Recording | Stenosis for each segment, and any thrombus, rupture, erosion, or dissection |
    | Myocardium | Bread-loaf the ventricles in transverse slices about 1 cm thick, from apex to about 2 cm below the atrioventricular valves |
    | Sampling | Each coronary territory, any lesion and its border, and the papillary muscles |

  bottom_line: |-
    Infarct morphology evolves predictably from invisible injury through coagulative necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, macrophage phagocytosis, granulation tissue, and scar; healing begins at the margins, dating is inherently approximate, and the infarct should be dated by integrating the most advanced representative changes with the overall picture.
- heading: Reperfusion
  question: |-
    How does restoring coronary flow differ between the acute setting of myocardial infarction and the chronic setting of stable coronary disease, and how does each change the morphology and function of the myocardium?
  content: |-
    Restoring coronary flow has different aims, time scales, and consequences in two settings. In the **acute setting**, an artery has just occluded, and reperfusion is a race against the wavefront of necrosis. In the **chronic setting**, myocardium has been underperfused for months or years by a fixed stenosis, and revascularization aims to relieve ischemia and recover dysfunctional but viable muscle. The two settings should not be confused: the evidence, the tissue response, and the morphology differ.

    **Acute and chronic settings compared**

    | Feature | Acute reperfusion | Chronic revascularization |
    |---|---|---|
    | Clinical setting | STEMI; high-risk NSTE-ACS | Chronic coronary syndrome (stable angina); ischemic cardiomyopathy |
    | Underlying lesion | Acute plaque change with occlusive or near-occlusive thrombus | Fixed, often multivessel, stenosis |
    | State of the myocardium | Evolving necrosis in the area at risk | Viable but hypoperfused (hibernating) myocardium, scar, or both |
    | Time frame | Minutes to hours; salvage falls steeply after the first hours | Elective; weeks to months |
    | Goal | Salvage the outer area at risk and limit infarct size | Relieve angina; recover hibernating myocardium; improve survival in selected anatomy |
    | Methods | Primary PCI (preferred); fibrinolysis if PCI is not timely; CABG rarely (anatomy or mechanical complication) | PCI or CABG, chosen by anatomy, left ventricular function, diabetes, and surgical risk |
    | Main tissue risk | Reperfusion injury: hemorrhage, contraction band necrosis, microvascular obstruction | Procedural infarction; later restenosis or graft failure |
    | Reversible dysfunction | Stunned myocardium (recovers over hours to days) | Hibernating myocardium (recovers over weeks to months after revascularization) |

    **Acute setting: reperfusion of an occluded artery**

    The objective is to salvage ischemic myocardium that is not yet necrotic, chiefly the outer, epicardial portion of the area at risk. Time is myocardium: the earlier flow is restored, the greater the salvage. Reperfusion is indicated within 12 hours of symptom onset in STEMI, and later if ischemia is ongoing. Reperfusion before irreversible injury has developed can prevent necrosis entirely; as the wavefront of necrosis progresses over the following hours, the amount of salvageable muscle declines steeply, but late reperfusion can still relieve ongoing ischemia, limit infarct size in selected patients, and reduce remodeling.

    Restoring flow after prolonged ischemia can itself add injury, known as reperfusion injury.

    | Mechanism | Pathophysiology | Consequence |
    |---|---|---|
    | Mitochondrial dysfunction | Opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore; release of cytochrome c and other pro-apoptotic proteins | Apoptosis and necrosis |
    | Myocyte hypercontracture | ATP restored in cells with high cytosolic calcium drives unregulated actin-myosin contraction | Contraction band necrosis |
    | Reactive oxygen species | Oxygen influx generates free radicals | Lipid and protein oxidation; membrane damage |
    | Leukocytes and no-reflow | Neutrophil plugging, endothelial swelling, microemboli, proteases | Microvascular obstruction |
    | Platelets and complement | Microthrombosis and inflammation amplify endothelial injury | Extension of ischemic damage |

    Reperfusion changes the morphology of the infarct, so standard dating tables apply less well.

    | Feature | Non-reperfused infarct | Reperfused infarct |
    |---|---|---|
    | Gross appearance | Pale or mottled, then yellow-tan | Often hemorrhagic, from vascular leakage and microvascular rupture |
    | Hallmark lesion | Coagulative necrosis | Contraction band necrosis (dense transverse eosinophilic bands of hypercontracted sarcomeres) |
    | Distribution | Transmural if occlusion persists | Often subendocardial, because the outer wall has been salvaged |
    | Inflammation and healing | Follow the standard timeline | Arrive earlier and progress faster |
    | Microvasculature | Usually intact at the margin | Obstruction (no-reflow); distal embolization of plaque or thrombus debris causing micro-infarcts |

    Stunned myocardium is the characteristic functional state of the acute setting. After brief, severe ischemia followed by reperfusion, perfusion is restored but contraction remains depressed and recovers spontaneously over hours to days. Left ventricular dysfunction early after primary PCI may therefore improve, and left ventricular ejection fraction should be reassessed before a primary-prevention defibrillator is considered (usually at least 40 days after infarction, or 90 days after revascularization).

    **Chronic setting: revascularization of chronically ischemic myocardium**

    The objective is to relieve ischemia in myocardium supplied by a fixed stenosis. Unlike the acute setting, there is no evolving infarct to salvage.
    The main questions are whether symptoms justify intervention and whether the dysfunctional myocardium is viable.

    Hibernating myocardium is the characteristic state of the chronic setting.
    Persistently reduced flow leads to an adaptive down-regulation of metabolism and contraction that preserves myocyte viability. Repeated episodes of stunning may contribute. Function recovers over weeks to months after revascularization, and the longer the dysfunction lasts, the less complete recovery becomes.

    | Feature | Hibernating myocardium |
    |---|---|
    | Morphology | Viable myocytes with loss of sarcomeres (myolysis), accumulation of glycogen, features of dedifferentiation, and increasing interstitial fibrosis over time |
    | Detection | Viability imaging: dobutamine echocardiography, positron emission tomography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (thin, densely scarred segments are unlikely to recover) |
    | Clinical relevance | Distinguishes recoverable dysfunction from scar in ischemic cardiomyopathy |

    Viability identifies potentially recoverable myocardium, but viability alone
    does not establish a survival benefit from revascularization; the benefit
    depends on coronary anatomy, left ventricular function, symptoms, surgical
    risk, and the revascularization strategy.

    Randomized evidence has refined the role of chronic revascularization. In
    stable coronary disease with moderate or severe ischemia, an initial
    invasive strategy did not reduce the primary composite outcome or all-cause
    mortality over the main trial follow-up, although it improved
    angina-related health status in symptomatic patients (ISCHEMIA). In ischemic
    left ventricular dysfunction, PCI added to optimal medical therapy did not
    reduce death or heart failure hospitalization (REVIVED-BCIS2), whereas CABG
    improved long-term survival in patients with ejection fraction of 35% or
    less (STICH extension study). Revascularization in the chronic setting is
    therefore chosen mainly for persistent symptoms and for anatomy with proven
    prognostic benefit, such as significant left main disease.

    Revascularized hearts have their own pathology, which pathologists meet at
    autopsy.

    | Lesion | Timing | Morphology |
    |---|---|---|
    | Stent thrombosis | Days to months, occasionally years | Occlusive thrombus within the stent |
    | In-stent restenosis | Months | Neointimal hyperplasia of smooth muscle cells and matrix; late neoatherosclerosis |
    | Saphenous vein graft failure | Early thrombosis; later intimal hyperplasia and atherosclerosis | About half of vein grafts are occluded by 10 years; atheroma in grafts is friable and embolizes |
    | Internal mammary artery graft | Rarely fails | More than 90% remain patent at 10 years |

    **Stunned and hibernating myocardium compared**

    | Feature | Stunned | Hibernating |
    |---|---|---|
    | Setting | Acute: brief severe ischemia, then reperfusion | Chronic: sustained or repeated hypoperfusion |
    | Perfusion | Restored | Persistently reduced |
    | Function | Transiently depressed | Adaptively down-regulated |
    | Recovery | Spontaneous, over hours to days | After revascularization, over weeks to months |
    | Clinical implication | Reassess LVEF before defibrillator decisions | Viability testing guides revascularization |

    Hemorrhage and contraction bands in a non-territorial pattern should raise
    consideration of resuscitation or catecholamine effect before they are
    attributed to reperfusion.

  bottom_line: |-
    Acute reperfusion salvages the outer area at risk but causes hemorrhage, contraction band necrosis, and faster healing, with stunning as the reversible dysfunction; chronic revascularization can recover hibernating myocardium but improves survival only in selected patients.
- heading: Management
  question: |-
    What is the physiologic rationale for initial therapy in acute myocardial infarction, and how is the reperfusion strategy chosen?
  content: |-
    **Initial therapy**

    | Therapy | Rationale | Practical points |
    |---|---|---|
    | Oxygen | Improve supply | Only if oxygen saturation is below 90% or there is respiratory distress; routine oxygen gives no benefit |
    | Nitrates | Relieve pain and spasm; reduce preload | Avoid in hypotension, right ventricular infarction, and recent phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use |
    | Antiplatelet therapy | Block platelet activation | Aspirin loading dose, then low dose, plus a P2Y12 inhibitor for most patients with presumed ACS; the choice and timing depend on PCI strategy, bleeding risk, and clinical setting. Dual antiplatelet therapy is commonly continued for about 12 months when bleeding risk permits, with individualized shortening or extension |
    | Anticoagulation | Block thrombin generation | Parenteral anticoagulation is generally used in patients with presumed ACS or type 1 MI, with the agent selected according to invasive strategy, renal function, bleeding risk, and reperfusion approach; patients may be reclassified (for example, type 2 MI or MINOCA) after further assessment |
    | Beta-blocker | Reduce oxygen demand; reduce arrhythmia | Oral, within 24 hours, if there is no heart failure, low-output state, shock risk, bradycardia, or atrioventricular block |
    | Monitoring and supportive care | Detect and treat arrhythmia; correct precipitants | Continuous ECG monitoring; treat ventricular fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, and atrioventricular block; correct anemia, hypoxemia, pain, and anxiety |

    **Reperfusion strategy in STEMI**

    | Strategy | When used | Targets and follow-up |
    |---|---|---|
    | Primary PCI (preferred) | Achievable within 120 minutes of first medical contact | First-medical-contact-to-device within 120 minutes is the key decision threshold (shorter targets, such as ≤90 minutes, remain appropriate for directly presenting patients where achievable); radial access and culprit-vessel stenting; complete revascularization of significant non-culprit disease is generally considered in hemodynamically stable patients, with timing and strategy individualized |
    | Fibrinolysis | PCI not achievable within 120 minutes, and symptoms within 12 hours | Administer a fibrin-specific agent promptly when indicated, followed by early angiography and PCI in a pharmaco-invasive strategy (about 2 to 24 hours after successful lysis) |
    | Rescue PCI | Failed fibrinolysis | Less than 50% ST resolution at 60–90 minutes |

    |---|---|---|
    | Very high | Immediate (<2 hours) | Shock, refractory pain, life-threatening arrhythmia, mechanical complication |
    | High | Early (<24 hours) | Confirmed NSTEMI, dynamic ST changes |

    | ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker | Especially LVEF ≤40%, heart failure, diabetes, or hypertension |
    | Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist | LVEF ≤40% with heart failure or diabetes |

- heading: Complications
  question: |-
    How does the timing of each complication of myocardial infarction relate to the underlying morphology?
  content: |-
    Complications of myocardial infarction follow a timeline anchored to infarct
    morphology.

    | Time after infarction | Complication | Morphologic substrate |
    |---|---|---|
    | Minutes to 1 hour | Sudden death from ventricular fibrillation | Electrical instability of ischemic muscle |
    | Hours to days | Arrhythmia, pump failure, cardiogenic shock, right ventricular infarction | Necrosis and loss of contractile mass |
    | Days 1–3 | Fibrinous pericarditis | Transmural necrosis inflaming the epicardium |
    | Days 3–7 | Free wall, septal, or papillary muscle rupture | Maximal softening before collagen deposition |
    | Days to weeks | Mural thrombus; remodeling; Dressler syndrome | Stasis and endocardial injury; infarct expansion; autoimmunity |
    | Weeks to years | True aneurysm; chronic heart failure; scar-related ventricular tachycardia | Thin collagenous scar; remodeling |

    **Immediate and early complications**

    | Complication | Features | Management |
    |---|---|---|
    | Sudden death and arrhythmia | Many STEMI deaths occur before hospital, mostly from ventricular fibrillation in the first hour. Arrhythmias occur in up to about 90% of infarcts: sinus bradycardia, atrioventricular block, supraventricular tachycardia, premature ventricular complexes, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole. | Continuous monitoring; defibrillation; risk is highest in the first hour and falls thereafter |
    | Left ventricular failure | Proportional to infarct size; hypotension, pulmonary congestion, edema | Reperfusion; heart failure therapy |
    | Cardiogenic shock | Associated with a large infarct burden, particularly extensive left ventricular infarction; high mortality | Urgent revascularization; hemodynamic support |
    | Right ventricular infarction | Proximal RCA occlusion with inferior or posterior infarction; hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, clear lungs, sometimes Kussmaul sign | Preload-dependent: avoid nitrates and diuretics; cautious fluid administration when hypotensive and preload-dependent; monitor for atrioventricular block |

    **Mechanical complications (myocardial rupture)**

    | Type | Consequence | Clinical clue |
    |---|---|---|
    | Free wall rupture | Hemopericardium and tamponade | Sudden pulseless electrical activity or shock |
    | Septal rupture | Acute ventricular septal defect with left-to-right shunt | New harsh holosystolic murmur and thrill |
    | Papillary muscle rupture | Acute severe mitral regurgitation | Flash pulmonary edema; murmur may be soft |

    Rupture occurs classically on days 3 to 7, at the time of maximal softening,
    but may occur earlier after late reperfusion. Its incidence has fallen below
    1% in the era of primary PCI, but mortality remains very high. Risk factors
    include older age, first infarction (no collaterals), large transmural
    infarction, and delayed or absent reperfusion.

    **Pericarditis, thrombus, and aneurysm**

    | Complication | Timing | Pathology | Clinical features |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Early pericarditis | Days 1–3 | Fibrinous or fibrinohemorrhagic, over a transmural infarct | Pleuritic pain, friction rub; usually self-limited |
    | Mural thrombus | Days to weeks | Stasis over akinetic segments, endocardial injury, dilation | Systemic embolism, such as stroke; anticoagulate when present |
    | True aneurysm | Weeks to months | Wide-necked outpouching lined by thin scar | Heart failure, ventricular tachycardia, mural thrombus; rupture is rare |
    | Pseudoaneurysm | Days to weeks | Contained free wall rupture; narrow neck; wall of pericardium and thrombus | High risk of rupture; urgent surgery |
    | Dressler syndrome | About 2–8 weeks | Autoimmune pericarditis from antibodies to myocardial or pericardial antigens | Fever, pleuritic pain, effusion; now rare in the reperfusion era |

    **Late complications: remodeling and heart failure**

    Infarct expansion stretches and thins the necrotic segment and dilates the
    chamber. Non-infarcted myocardium undergoes compensatory hypertrophy,
    interstitial fibrosis, and a more spherical shape. These changes are
    initially adaptive but ultimately raise wall stress and oxygen demand.
    Cumulative loss of contractile mass, remodeling, and ischemia of viable
    muscle produce ischemic cardiomyopathy. Therapies that limit remodeling in
    heart failure with reduced ejection fraction are ACE inhibitors or
    angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors,
    beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and sodium-glucose
    cotransporter 2 inhibitors.

    **Prognosis and prevention**

    Prognosis varies substantially with infarct size and location, left
    ventricular function, heart failure or shock, age, renal disease, diabetes,
    previous infarction, and access to timely reperfusion. Contemporary outcomes
    differ markedly between STEMI and NSTEMI populations and across healthcare
    systems, so single numerical mortality figures are not informative here.
    Primary prevention means control of risk factors before a first event;
    secondary prevention combines smoking cessation, cardiac rehabilitation,
    lipid lowering to target, antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockade, ACE
    inhibition or angiotensin receptor blockade, and blood pressure and glycemic
    control.

  bottom_line: |-
    Complications of myocardial infarction follow a timeline anchored to infarct morphology: arrhythmia in the first hour, pump failure in the first days, rupture at days three to seven during maximal softening, and aneurysm, thrombus, and scar-related arrhythmia in the weeks to years thereafter.
- heading: Synthesis
  question: |-
    Can the pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical course of myocardial infarction be integrated into a single framework, and how does that framework apply to individual patients?
  content: |-
    Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance of oxygen supply and demand, and every
    therapy either restores supply or reduces demand. Stable and vulnerable
    plaques are different lesions: stable plaques cause predictable angina,
    whereas vulnerable plaques undergo rupture or erosion with thrombosis, often
    at a site that was not critically stenotic. Necrosis begins in the vulnerable
    subendocardium after a variable interval of severe ischemia and advances
    toward the epicardium over the following hours, so timing determines salvage.
    Morphology dates the infarct, albeit approximately, and predicts its
    complications. Acute reperfusion salvages muscle but changes the histologic
    picture, whereas chronic revascularization targets hibernating myocardium
    behind a fixed stenosis.

    **Case-based review**

    | Case | Most likely diagnosis | Morphologic correlate | Key management |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 64-year-old man, late-presenting anterior STEMI without reperfusion; on day 5, sudden hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, pulseless electrical activity | Free wall rupture with tamponade | Days 3–7: disintegrating myofibres, macrophage phagocytosis, little collagen (maximal softening) | Emergency pericardial decompression and surgery |
    | 70-year-old man, inferior STEMI; hypotension after sublingual nitroglycerin; clear lungs, raised jugular venous pressure | Right ventricular infarction (proximal RCA occlusion) | Necrosis of the right ventricular free wall and inferior left ventricle | Stop nitrates; cautious intravenous fluids; urgent reperfusion; monitor for atrioventricular block |
    | 55-year-old woman dies 4 days after primary PCI; grossly hemorrhagic infarct; dense transverse eosinophilic bands in myocytes | Reperfused infarct with contraction band necrosis | Calcium influx into injured myocytes with restored ATP causes hypercontracture; hemorrhage reflects microvascular injury | Interpret dating with caution; exclude resuscitation and catecholamine effect |
    | 78-year-old woman in septic shock, heart rate 140/min; rising and falling hs-troponin; stable non-obstructive plaques on angiography | Type 2 myocardial infarction | Supply-demand mismatch without acute atherothrombosis | Treat the precipitant; ACS antithrombotic therapy is not routinely indicated solely because a patient has type 2 MI, and management should be individualized to coronary findings and thrombotic risk |
    | 66-year-old man with multivessel disease, LVEF 30%, and akinetic anterior wall that thickens with low-dose dobutamine | Hibernating myocardium in ischemic cardiomyopathy | Viable myocytes with myolysis and glycogen accumulation; limited fibrosis | Heart-team assessment of revascularization, with CABG considered when coronary anatomy and clinical characteristics indicate a prognostic benefit, particularly in suitable patients with severe LV dysfunction; full heart failure therapy |

take_home_messages:
- |-
  Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance of oxygen supply and demand; therapy either restores supply or reduces demand.
- |-
  Stable and vulnerable plaques are different lesions; acute coronary syndromes arise from acute plaque change with thrombosis, often at a site that was not critically stenotic.
- |-
  Irreversible injury may begin after roughly 20 to 30 minutes of severe, sustained ischemia, and necrosis advances from the vulnerable subendocardium toward the epicardium as a variable wavefront; timing sets salvage.
- |-
  Morphology dates the infarct, albeit approximately: neutrophils at 1 to 3 days, macrophages at 3 to 10 days, granulation tissue at 1 to 2 weeks, scar by 2 months.
- |-
  Morphology predicts complications: softening at days 3 to 7 explains rupture; thin scar explains aneurysm, thrombus, and ventricular tachycardia.
- |-
  Acute reperfusion salvages muscle but produces hemorrhage, contraction bands, and faster, atypical healing; stunned myocardium recovers over hours to days.
- |-
  Chronic revascularization treats fixed stenosis and can recover hibernating myocardium, but viability alone does not establish a survival benefit, which is seen only in selected patients.
bottom_line: |-
  Ischemic heart disease is a supply-demand imbalance; acute plaque change drives infarction, morphology dates the infarct approximately and predicts its complications, acute reperfusion salvages muscle while altering the histologic picture, and chronic revascularization targets viable hibernating myocardium.
