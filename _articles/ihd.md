question: >-
  How does myocardial ischemia progress from coronary plaque to myocardial
  infarction and scar, and how do morphologic features guide the dating of
  infarction, recognition of complications, and clinical management?

background: >-
  Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and
  demand. It is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for
  approximately nine million deaths each year, although age-adjusted mortality
  in high-income countries has fallen by more than half since the 1960s
  through prevention, better therapy, and lifestyle change. The principal cause
  is obstructive atherosclerosis of the epicardial coronary arteries. Stable
  angina and acute coronary syndromes are not points on a single scale of
  stenosis severity: they are distinct clinical entities with different plaque
  biology, presentation, and management. This framework follows the disease
  from etiology and plaque biology through the ischemic myocardium, the
  morphologic evolution of infarction, reperfusion in the acute and chronic
  settings, initial management, complications, and synthesis. It is written
  for postgraduate pathology review.

sections:

  - question: >-
      What is myocardial ischemia, and what is the spectrum of clinical
      syndromes it produces?
    text: >-
      Myocardial ischemia is inadequate perfusion relative to metabolic
      demand. It differs from pure hypoxemia because substrate delivery and
      removal of metabolites are also impaired, so ischemic injury evolves
      faster than hypoxic injury alone. The principal cause is obstructive
      atherosclerosis of the epicardial coronary arteries, which is why
      ischemic heart disease is often used synonymously with coronary artery
      disease. Lesions favour the proximal segments and branch points of the
      left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex (LCX), and right
      coronary (RCA) arteries.

      **Causes of reduced supply**

      | Category | Examples |
      |---|---|
      | Atherosclerosis (>90% of cases) | Fixed stenosis; acute plaque change with thrombosis |
      | Vasospasm | Prinzmetal angina; drug-induced (cocaine, ephedrine) |
      | Coronary embolism | Atrial fibrillation, left ventricular thrombus, endocarditis, prosthetic valves |
      | Vasculitis and dissection | Kawasaki disease; spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) |
      | Microvascular disease | Diabetes, hypertension |
      | Low perfusion pressure or O₂ content | Shock, hypotension, hypoxemia, anemia |

      **Precipitants that tip the balance**

      | Precipitant | Effect on demand | Effect on supply |
      |---|---|---|
      | Tachycardia | ↑ Heart rate increases O₂ demand | ↓ Shorter diastole reduces left ventricular perfusion time |
      | Myocardial hypertrophy | ↑ Muscle mass and wall stress | ↓ Capillary density relative to muscle mass |
      | Anemia, hypoxemia | — | ↓ Arterial O₂ content |
      | Systemic hypotension | — | ↓ Coronary perfusion pressure |

      Tachycardia is doubly harmful because it acts on both sides of the
      balance.

      **Clinical spectrum**

      | Syndrome | Pathophysiology | Outcome |
      |---|---|---|
      | Angina pectoris (stable, Prinzmetal, unstable) | Transient ischemia without necrosis | Chest pain; reversible |
      | Myocardial infarction | Prolonged ischemia with irreversible myocyte necrosis | Acute infarct; may heal as scar |
      | Chronic ischemic heart disease | Progressive myocyte loss, scarring, remodeling | Ischemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure |
      | Sudden cardiac death | Lethal arrhythmia from acute ischemia or scar | Death within about one hour of symptoms |

      Unstable angina, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and
      ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) together form the acute
      coronary syndromes (ACS).

    bottom_line: >-
      Ischemic heart disease is a supply-demand imbalance; the principal
      cause is epicardial coronary atherosclerosis, but precipitants and
      non-atherosclerotic causes must be considered in every case.

  - question: >-
      What distinguishes a stable atherosclerotic plaque from a vulnerable
      plaque, and how does acute plaque change produce an acute coronary
      syndrome?
    text: >-
      There are two separate routes to myocardial ischemia, and they should
      not be conflated.

      | Feature | Chronic fixed stenosis | Acute plaque change |
      |---|---|---|
      | Plaque | Stable: thick fibrous cap, small lipid core, little inflammation | Vulnerable: thin fibrous cap, large necrotic core, heavy inflammation |
      | Mechanism | Gradual luminal narrowing | Rupture, fissure, ulceration, or erosion with thrombosis |
      | Degree of stenosis | ≥70%: ischemia on exertion; ≥90%: ischemia at rest | Often only moderate (<70%) before the event |
      | Collaterals | Have time to develop and limit ischemia | Little time to develop |
      | Clinical result | Stable angina; chronic ischemic heart disease | Unstable angina, NSTEMI, STEMI, sudden death |

      A stable plaque may remain quiescent for years and does not become an
      acute coronary syndrome simply by enlarging. Stenosis severity poorly
      predicts which plaque will rupture, and the lesion that causes angina
      and the lesion that later ruptures are frequently different plaques in
      the same patient. Complete occlusion typically produces a transmural
      infarct with ST elevation; partial or transient occlusion produces
      unstable angina or a subendocardial infarct without ST elevation.
      Statins stabilize plaques by reducing inflammation and improving
      endothelial function, a benefit that extends beyond lowering LDL
      cholesterol.

      **Plaque rupture and plaque erosion**

      | Feature | Rupture | Erosion |
      |---|---|---|
      | Share of coronary thrombi | Majority | About one-quarter to one-third |
      | Fibrous cap | Thin (<65 µm) and disrupted | Intact; endothelium denuded |
      | Plaque | Large necrotic lipid core, many macrophages | Little or no lipid core; proteoglycan- and smooth muscle cell-rich |
      | Thrombus | Fibrin- and red cell-rich ("red") | Platelet-rich ("white"), with neutrophils and neutrophil extracellular traps |
      | Typical patient | Older men with classic risk factors | Younger patients, women, smokers; often non-ST-elevation ACS |

      At autopsy, cutting the culprit segment at 3 to 5 mm intervals and
      taking step sections through the base of the thrombus helps separate
      rupture from erosion.

      **The vulnerable patient**

      Vulnerability extends beyond the plaque. Inflammation is the common
      thread across all three domains, which explains why two patients with
      identical angiograms can have very different outcomes and provides the
      rationale for anti-inflammatory strategies in coronary artery disease.

      | Domain | Determinants |
      |---|---|
      | Vulnerable plaque | Inflammation; extent, severity, and location of disease |
      | Thrombogenic blood | Inflammation; comorbidities (diabetes, chronic kidney disease); smoking; genetic background |
      | Vulnerable myocardium | Duration and extent of ischemia; individual susceptibility; microvascular injury |

      **Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction**

      Myocardial injury is a cardiac troponin value above the 99th
      percentile upper reference limit; it is acute if there is a rise or
      fall. Myocardial infarction is acute myocardial injury plus clinical
      evidence of ischemia: symptoms, new ischemic electrocardiographic
      changes, new pathological Q waves, imaging evidence of new loss of
      viable myocardium or a new regional wall-motion abnormality, or
      coronary thrombus at angiography or autopsy.

      | Type | Mechanism | Typical setting |
      |---|---|---|
      | 1 | Atherothrombotic plaque disruption with thrombus | Classic spontaneous ACS |
      | 2 | Supply-demand mismatch without acute atherothrombosis | Sepsis, anemia, tachyarrhythmia, hypotension, spasm, SCAD |
      | 3 | Cardiac death with suggestive symptoms before biomarkers are available | Sudden death; often confirmed at autopsy |
      | 4 | Related to percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI): 4a procedural, 4b stent thrombosis, 4c restenosis | After PCI |
      | 5 | Related to coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) | After CABG |

      **Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA)**

      MINOCA is a working diagnosis. Myocarditis and takotsubo syndrome must
      be excluded, usually by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, before a
      cause is assigned.

      | Category | Examples |
      |---|---|
      | Fixed plaque unmasked by imbalance | Stable plaque with a superimposed supply-demand mismatch |
      | Non-atherosclerotic coronary disease | Spasm, small-vessel disease, vasculitis, dissection, embolism, congenital anomalies, fibromuscular dysplasia, iatrogenic causes, PCI-related no-reflow |
      | Non-coronary cardiac imbalance | Sustained tachyarrhythmia or bradyarrhythmia; left ventricular hypertrophy or dilatation |
      | Extracardiac imbalance | Respiratory failure, severe anemia, hypovolemic or septic shock |
      | Other | Sickle cell disease; amyloidosis involving intramural vessels; catecholamine excess (cocaine, pheochromocytoma) |

      At autopsy, sample intramural vessels widely and consider elastic
      stains for dissection and vasculitis and Congo red for amyloid.

      **The thrombotic cascade and its drug targets**

      | Step | Event | Drug target |
      |---|---|---|
      | 1. Plaque disruption | Collagen and necrotic core exposed | Statins (plaque stabilization) |
      | 2. Platelet activation | Adhesion and aggregation; release of thromboxane A2, ADP, serotonin | Aspirin (thromboxane A2); P2Y12 inhibitors (ADP); glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (final common pathway) |
      | 3. Coagulation | Tissue factor activates the cascade, generating thrombin and fibrin | Anticoagulants |
      | 4. Occlusion | Thrombus occludes the lumen within minutes | Fibrinolysis; PCI |
      | 5. Necrosis | Irreversible injury after 20 to 30 minutes of severe ischemia | Early reperfusion |

      Thrombus is found in about 90% of transmural infarcts examined early.

    bottom_line: >-
      Stable and vulnerable plaques are different lesions with different
      biology; acute coronary syndromes arise from acute plaque change with
      thrombosis, often at a site that was not critically stenotic before
      the event.

  - question: >-
      Once coronary flow is interrupted, how quickly does the myocardium
      become irreversibly injured, and in what pattern does necrosis spread?
    text: >-
      **Myocardial response to ischemia**

      | Time after onset | Event | Reversible? |
      |---|---|---|
      | Seconds | Switch to anaerobic glycolysis; ATP falls, lactate rises | Yes |
      | < 1 minute | Loss of contractility | Yes |
      | Minutes | Myofibril relaxation, glycogen loss, cell and mitochondrial swelling | Yes |
      | 20 to 30 minutes | Irreversible injury begins | No |
      | 6 to 12 hours | Necrosis of the whole area at risk, if not reperfused | No |

      The earliest necrosis occurs in the subendocardium, which is perfused
      last, bears the highest wall stress, and has the fewest collaterals.
      Necrosis then advances as a wavefront from subendocardium toward
      epicardium over hours. Reperfusion within the first few hours salvages
      the still-viable outer wall, which is the basis of door-to-balloon
      targets.

      **Patterns of infarction**

      | Pattern | Mechanism | Distribution | Common causes |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Transmural | Complete, persistent occlusion of an epicardial artery | Full wall thickness within one coronary territory | Plaque rupture with occlusive thrombus |
      | Subendocardial (non-transmural), regional | Partial or transient occlusion | Inner one-third to one-half of the wall in one territory | Lysis of an occlusive thrombus; early reperfusion |
      | Subendocardial, circumferential | Global hypoperfusion | Inner wall around the whole ventricle, crossing territories | Prolonged shock or hypotension |
      | Multifocal micro-infarction | Disease of small intramural vessels | Microscopic, scattered | Microemboli, vasculitis, spasm, catecholamine toxicity |

      A circumferential subendocardial infarct that is not confined to one
      territory points to global hypoperfusion rather than a single culprit
      artery. Catecholamine excess, as with cocaine, can cause sudden death
      or, with repeated insults, chronic ischemic cardiomyopathy.

      **Coronary territories (right-dominant heart)**

      | Artery | Share of infarcts | Territory | ECG leads |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | LAD | 40–50% | Anterior left ventricular wall near the apex, anterior two-thirds of the septum, apex circumferentially | V1–V4 (anterior, anteroseptal, apical) |
      | RCA | 30–40% | Right ventricular free wall, inferior and posterior left ventricle, posterior one-third of the septum | II, III, aVF; V4R if right ventricle involved |
      | LCX | 15–20% | Lateral left ventricular wall except the apex | I, aVL, V5–V6; inferoposterior if left-dominant |
      | Other | <5% | Left main, diagonal, and obtuse marginal branches | Variable; left main occlusion is extensive and high-risk |
      | Isolated right ventricle | 1–3% | Right ventricular free wall | V4R |
      | Atrium | Rare | Usually with RCA or LCX occlusion | Atrial arrhythmias |

      Inferior STEMI with hypotension and clear lungs suggests right
      ventricular infarction. Record right-sided leads, avoid nitrates, and
      give fluids cautiously.
    tables: []
    bottom_line: >-
      Necrosis begins subendocardially at 20 to 30 minutes and advances as a
      wavefront to the epicardium over 6 to 12 hours; timing determines
      salvage, and the pattern of infarction reflects the underlying
      mechanism.

  - question: >-
      Can an infarct be dated reliably from its gross and microscopic
      appearance, and what are the pitfalls in dating it at autopsy?
    text: >-
      Infarct morphology evolves in a predictable sequence that allows
      dating. The timings below apply to infarcts that have not been
      reperfused; reperfused infarcts evolve faster and look different (see
      Reperfusion).

      **Gross and microscopic evolution**

      | Age | Gross | Light microscopy |
      |---|---|---|
      | 0 to ½ hour | None | None; electron microscopy shows reversible injury |
      | ½ to 4 hours | None (tetrazolium stains detect infarcts ≥2–3 hours old) | Usually none; wavy fibres at the border |
      | 4 to 24 hours | Dark mottling | Early coagulative necrosis: hypereosinophilic cytoplasm, pyknotic nuclei; edema; focal hemorrhage; neutrophils appear by 12–24 hours |
      | 1 to 3 days | Mottling with a yellow-tan centre | Loss of nuclei and cross-striations; brisk neutrophilic infiltrate |
      | 3 to 7 days | Hyperemic border; central yellow-tan softening | Disintegration of dead myofibres; dying neutrophils; macrophages at the border |
      | 7 to 10 days | Maximally yellow-tan and soft; depressed red-tan margins | Well-developed phagocytosis; early granulation tissue at the margins |
      | 10 to 14 days | Red-gray depressed borders | Established granulation tissue with new vessels and collagen |
      | 2 to 8 weeks | Gray-white scar progressing from border to core | Increasing collagen, decreasing cellularity |
      | > 2 months | Dense, firm gray-white scar; wall may be thinned | Dense collagen with few fibroblasts and vessels |

      Healing is centripetal, so the centre of a large infarct can look
      younger than its edge; date the infarct from its most advanced area.
      Cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated and do not regenerate,
      and once healed a scar cannot be dated precisely.

      **Histologic dating parameters (H&E)**

      | Feature | Earliest | Full development | Decrease or disappearance |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Stretched or wavy fibres | 1–2 hours | — | — |
      | Hypereosinophilia (coagulative necrosis) | 1–3 hours | 1–3 days, with loss of striations | Disintegration after 3 days |
      | Interstitial edema | 4–12 hours | — | — |
      | Nuclear changes | 12–24 hours (pyknosis, karyorrhexis) | 1–3 days (loss of nuclei) | Depends on infarct size |
      | Neutrophil infiltration | 12–24 hours | 1–3 days | 5–7 days |
      | Neutrophil karyorrhexis | 1.5–2 days | 3–5 days | — |
      | Macrophages and lymphocytes | 3–5 days | 5–10 days, including siderophages | 10 days to 2 months |
      | Vessel and endothelial sprouts* | 5–10 days | 10 days to 4 weeks | After 4 weeks; some large dilated vessels persist |
      | Fibroblasts and young collagen* | 5–10 days | 2–4 weeks | After 4 weeks, depending on infarct size |
      | Dense fibrosis | 4 weeks | 2–3 months | Permanent |

      *Often summarized together as granulation tissue, which is maximal at
      2 to 3 weeks. Adapted from Michaud et al., 2020.

      **Interpretive pitfalls**

      | Finding | Caution |
      |---|---|
      | Wavy fibres | Non-specific; also seen with agonal stretching and artefact. Never diagnose infarction on waviness alone. |
      | Hypereosinophilia | Weak evidence on its own; judge against adjacent myocardium on the same slide. |
      | Contraction band necrosis | Also caused by reperfusion, resuscitation or defibrillation, catecholamines, and handling artefact. |
      | Autolysis | Pale, fragmented myocytes; do not over-interpret. |
      | Death within about 4–6 hours | Morphology may be absent; rely on tetrazolium stains, the coronary lesion, and the history. |
      | Ancillary immunohistochemistry (C5b-9, C4d, fibronectin) | Can highlight early injury but is not fully validated. |

      A new friction rub on day two or three after a transmural infarct
      suggests early post-infarction pericarditis rather than Dressler
      syndrome.

      **Tetrazolium stains**

      | Aspect | Detail |
      |---|---|
      | Principle | Dehydrogenases and cofactors in viable myocardium reduce tetrazolium salts to a coloured formazan: red with triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC), blue-purple with nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT). Infarcted muscle has lost these enzymes and stays pale. |
      | Window | Infarcts at least 2–3 hours old, before changes are visible grossly or on H&E |
      | Technique | Fresh, unfixed transverse slices about 1 cm thick, incubated in buffered stain at 37 °C and photographed promptly |
      | Limitations | False positives with a long post-mortem interval (autolysis); fixation abolishes staining; reperfused infarcts stain patchily |
      | Practice | Stain before formalin fixation; sample the pale zone and its border for histology |

      **Autopsy approach to suspected myocardial infarction**

      | Step | Practice |
      |---|---|
      | Coronary arteries | Cut transversely at 3–5 mm intervals along the main epicardial arteries; decalcify heavily calcified segments rather than forcing the blade |
      | Recording | Stenosis for each segment, and any thrombus, rupture, erosion, or dissection |
      | Myocardium | Bread-loaf the ventricles in transverse slices about 1 cm thick, from apex to about 2 cm below the atrioventricular valves |
      | Sampling | Each coronary territory, any lesion and its border, and the papillary muscles |
    tables: []
    bottom_line: >-
      Infarct morphology evolves predictably from invisible injury through
      coagulative necrosis, neutrophilic infiltration, macrophage
      phagocytosis, granulation tissue, and scar; healing is centripetal and
      the infarct should be dated from its most advanced area.

  - question: >-
      How does restoring coronary flow differ between the acute setting of
      myocardial infarction and the chronic setting of stable coronary
      disease, and how does each change the morphology and function of the
      myocardium?
    text: >-
      Restoring coronary flow has different aims, time scales, and
      consequences in two settings. In the **acute setting**, an artery has
      just occluded, and reperfusion is a race against the wavefront of
      necrosis. In the **chronic setting**, myocardium has been
      underperfused for months or years by a fixed stenosis, and
      revascularization aims to relieve ischemia and recover dysfunctional
      but viable muscle. The two settings should not be confused: the
      evidence, the tissue response, and the morphology differ.

      **Acute and chronic settings compared**

      | Feature | Acute reperfusion | Chronic revascularization |
      |---|---|---|
      | Clinical setting | STEMI; high-risk NSTE-ACS | Chronic coronary syndrome (stable angina); ischemic cardiomyopathy |
      | Underlying lesion | Acute plaque change with occlusive or near-occlusive thrombus | Fixed, often multivessel, stenosis |
      | State of the myocardium | Evolving necrosis in the area at risk | Viable but hypoperfused (hibernating) myocardium, scar, or both |
      | Time frame | Minutes to hours; salvage falls steeply after the first hours | Elective; weeks to months |
      | Goal | Salvage the outer area at risk and limit infarct size | Relieve angina; recover hibernating myocardium; improve survival in selected anatomy |
      | Methods | Primary PCI (preferred); fibrinolysis if PCI is not timely; CABG rarely (anatomy or mechanical complication) | PCI or CABG, chosen by anatomy, left ventricular function, diabetes, and surgical risk |
      | Main tissue risk | Reperfusion injury: hemorrhage, contraction band necrosis, microvascular obstruction | Procedural infarction; later restenosis or graft failure |
      | Reversible dysfunction | Stunned myocardium (recovers over hours to days) | Hibernating myocardium (recovers over weeks to months after revascularization) |

      **Acute setting: reperfusion of an occluded artery**

      The objective is to salvage ischemic myocardium that is not yet
      necrotic, chiefly the outer, epicardial portion of the area at risk.
      Time is myocardium: the earlier flow is restored, the greater the
      salvage. Reperfusion is indicated within 12 hours of symptom onset in
      STEMI, and later if ischemia is ongoing. Reperfusion before about 20
      to 30 minutes can prevent necrosis entirely; after 6 to 12 hours
      little muscle remains to salvage.

      Restoring flow after prolonged ischemia can itself add injury, known
      as reperfusion injury.

      | Mechanism | Pathophysiology | Consequence |
      |---|---|---|
      | Mitochondrial dysfunction | Opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore; release of cytochrome c and other pro-apoptotic proteins | Apoptosis and necrosis |
      | Myocyte hypercontracture | ATP restored in cells with high cytosolic calcium drives unregulated actin-myosin contraction | Contraction band necrosis |
      | Reactive oxygen species | Oxygen influx generates free radicals | Lipid and protein oxidation; membrane damage |
      | Leukocytes and no-reflow | Neutrophil plugging, endothelial swelling, microemboli, proteases | Microvascular obstruction |
      | Platelets and complement | Microthrombosis and inflammation amplify endothelial injury | Extension of ischemic damage |

      Reperfusion changes the morphology of the infarct, so standard dating
      tables apply less well.

      | Feature | Non-reperfused infarct | Reperfused infarct |
      |---|---|---|
      | Gross appearance | Pale or mottled, then yellow-tan | Often hemorrhagic, from vascular leakage and microvascular rupture |
      | Hallmark lesion | Coagulative necrosis | Contraction band necrosis (dense transverse eosinophilic bands of hypercontracted sarcomeres) |
      | Distribution | Transmural if occlusion persists | Often subendocardial, because the outer wall has been salvaged |
      | Inflammation and healing | Follow the standard timeline | Arrive earlier and progress faster |
      | Microvasculature | Usually intact at the margin | Obstruction (no-reflow); distal embolization of plaque or thrombus debris causing micro-infarcts |

      Stunned myocardium is the characteristic functional state of the
      acute setting. After brief, severe ischemia followed by reperfusion,
      perfusion is restored but contraction remains depressed and recovers
      spontaneously over hours to days. Left ventricular dysfunction early
      after primary PCI may therefore improve, and left ventricular
      ejection fraction should be reassessed before a primary-prevention
      defibrillator is considered (usually at least 40 days after
      infarction, or 90 days after revascularization).

      **Chronic setting: revascularization of chronically ischemic myocardium**

      The objective is to relieve ischemia in myocardium supplied by a
      fixed stenosis. Unlike the acute setting, there is no evolving
      infarct to salvage. The main questions are whether symptoms justify
      intervention and whether the dysfunctional myocardium is viable.

      Hibernating myocardium is the characteristic state of the chronic
      setting. Persistently reduced flow leads to an adaptive
      down-regulation of metabolism and contraction that preserves myocyte
      viability. Repeated episodes of stunning may contribute. Function
      recovers over weeks to months after revascularization, and the longer
      the dysfunction lasts, the less complete recovery becomes.

      | Feature | Hibernating myocardium |
      |---|---|
      | Morphology | Viable myocytes with loss of sarcomeres (myolysis), accumulation of glycogen, features of dedifferentiation, and increasing interstitial fibrosis over time |
      | Detection | Viability imaging: dobutamine echocardiography, positron emission tomography, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (thin, densely scarred segments are unlikely to recover) |
      | Clinical relevance | Distinguishes recoverable dysfunction from scar in ischemic cardiomyopathy |

      Randomized evidence has refined the role of chronic
      revascularization. In stable coronary disease with moderate or severe
      ischemia, a routine invasive strategy did not reduce death or
      myocardial infarction compared with optimal medical therapy, although
      it improved angina (ISCHEMIA). In ischemic left ventricular
      dysfunction, PCI added to optimal medical therapy did not reduce
      death or heart failure hospitalization (REVIVED-BCIS2), whereas CABG
      improved long-term survival in patients with ejection fraction of 35%
      or less (STICH extension study). Revascularization in the chronic
      setting is therefore chosen mainly for persistent symptoms and for
      anatomy with proven prognostic benefit, such as significant left main
      disease.

      Revascularized hearts have their own pathology, which pathologists
      meet at autopsy.

      | Lesion | Timing | Morphology |
      |---|---|---|
      | Stent thrombosis | Days to months, occasionally years | Occlusive thrombus within the stent |
      | In-stent restenosis | Months | Neointimal hyperplasia of smooth muscle cells and matrix; late neoatherosclerosis |
      | Saphenous vein graft failure | Early thrombosis; later intimal hyperplasia and atherosclerosis | About half of vein grafts are occluded by 10 years; atheroma in grafts is friable and embolizes |
      | Internal mammary artery graft | Rarely fails | More than 90% remain patent at 10 years |

      **Stunned and hibernating myocardium compared**

      | Feature | Stunned | Hibernating |
      |---|---|---|
      | Setting | Acute: brief severe ischemia, then reperfusion | Chronic: sustained or repeated hypoperfusion |
      | Perfusion | Restored | Persistently reduced |
      | Function | Transiently depressed | Adaptively down-regulated |
      | Recovery | Spontaneous, over hours to days | After revascularization, over weeks to months |
      | Clinical implication | Reassess LVEF before defibrillator decisions | Viability testing guides revascularization |

      Hemorrhage and contraction bands in a non-territorial pattern should
      raise consideration of resuscitation or catecholamine effect before
      they are attributed to reperfusion.
    tables: []
    bottom_line: >-
      Acute reperfusion salvages the outer area at risk but causes
      hemorrhage, contraction band necrosis, and faster healing, with
      stunning as the reversible dysfunction; chronic revascularization can
      recover hibernating myocardium but improves survival only in selected
      patients.

  - question: >-
      What is the physiologic rationale for initial therapy in acute
      myocardial infarction, and how is the reperfusion strategy chosen?
    text: >-
      **Initial therapy**

      | Therapy | Rationale | Practical points |
      |---|---|---|
      | Oxygen | Improve supply | Only if oxygen saturation is below 90% or there is respiratory distress; routine oxygen gives no benefit |
      | Nitrates | Relieve pain and spasm; reduce preload | Avoid in hypotension, right ventricular infarction, and recent phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use |
      | Antiplatelet therapy | Block platelet activation | Aspirin loading dose, then low dose, plus a P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor or prasugrel preferred when PCI is planned); dual antiplatelet therapy for about 12 months by default |
      | Anticoagulation | Block thrombin generation | Parenteral anticoagulant for all: unfractionated heparin in primary PCI; enoxaparin, fondaparinux, or bivalirudin by context |
      | Beta-blocker | Reduce oxygen demand; reduce arrhythmia | Oral, within 24 hours, if there is no heart failure, low-output state, shock risk, bradycardia, or atrioventricular block |
      | Monitoring and supportive care | Detect and treat arrhythmia; correct precipitants | Continuous ECG monitoring; treat ventricular fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, and atrioventricular block; correct anemia, hypoxemia, pain, and anxiety |

      **Reperfusion strategy in STEMI**

      | Strategy | When used | Targets and follow-up |
      |---|---|---|
      | Primary PCI (preferred) | Achievable within 120 minutes of first medical contact | First-medical-contact-to-device ≤90 minutes (≤120 minutes with inter-hospital transfer); radial access and culprit-vessel stenting; complete revascularization of non-culprit lesions in most stable patients |
      | Fibrinolysis | PCI not achievable within 120 minutes, and symptoms within 12 hours | Fibrin-specific agent within 30 minutes of arrival (within 10 minutes of diagnosis in the European Society of Cardiology pathway); angiography 2–24 hours after successful lysis |
      | Rescue PCI | Failed fibrinolysis | Less than 50% ST resolution at 60–90 minutes |

      **Timing of angiography in non-ST-elevation ACS**

      | Risk | Timing | Examples |
      |---|---|---|
      | Very high | Immediate (<2 hours) | Shock, refractory pain, life-threatening arrhythmia, mechanical complication |
      | High | Early (<24 hours) | Confirmed NSTEMI, dynamic ST changes |

      **Secondary prevention after myocardial infarction**

      | Therapy | Indication |
      |---|---|
      | High-intensity statin (with ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitor if needed) | All patients, started early, titrated to LDL cholesterol target |
      | ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker | Especially LVEF ≤40%, heart failure, diabetes, or hypertension |
      | Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist | LVEF ≤40% with heart failure or diabetes |
      | Lifestyle and rehabilitation | Smoking cessation; cardiac rehabilitation; blood pressure and glycemic control |
    tables: []
    bottom_line: >-
      Initial management restores supply-demand balance, limits infarct
      size, and prevents complications; reperfusion strategy in
      ST-elevation myocardial infarction is time-critical and follows
      defined first-medical-contact targets.

  - question: >-
      How does the timing of each complication of myocardial infarction
      relate to the underlying morphology?
    text: >-
      Complications of myocardial infarction follow a timeline anchored to
      infarct morphology.

      | Time after infarction | Complication | Morphologic substrate |
      |---|---|---|
      | Minutes to 1 hour | Sudden death from ventricular fibrillation | Electrical instability of ischemic muscle |
      | Hours to days | Arrhythmia, pump failure, cardiogenic shock, right ventricular infarction | Necrosis and loss of contractile mass |
      | Days 1–3 | Fibrinous pericarditis | Transmural necrosis inflaming the epicardium |
      | Days 3–7 | Free wall, septal, or papillary muscle rupture | Maximal softening before collagen deposition |

      | Weeks to years | True aneurysm; chronic heart failure; scar-related ventricular tachycardia | Thin collagenous scar; remodeling |

      **Immediate and early complications**

      | Complication | Features | Management |
      |---|---|---|
      | Sudden death and arrhythmia | Many STEMI deaths occur before hospital, mostly from ventricular fibrillation in the first hour. Arrhythmias occur in up to about 90% of infarcts: sinus bradycardia, atrioventricular block, supraventricular tachycardia, premature ventricular complexes, ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, asystole. | Continuous monitoring; defibrillation; risk is highest in the first hour and falls thereafter |
      | Left ventricular failure | Proportional to infarct size; hypotension, pulmonary congestion, edema | Reperfusion; heart failure therapy |
      | Cardiogenic shock | About 10% of transmural infarcts, typically with ≥40% of the left ventricle infarcted | Urgent revascularization; hemodynamic support |
      | Right ventricular infarction | Proximal RCA occlusion with inferior or posterior infarction; hypotension, raised jugular venous pressure, clear lungs, sometimes Kussmaul sign | Preload-dependent: avoid nitrates and diuretics, give fluids, monitor for atrioventricular block |

      **Mechanical complications (myocardial rupture)**

      | Type | Consequence | Clinical clue |
      |---|---|---|
      | Free wall rupture | Hemopericardium and tamponade | Sudden pulseless electrical activity or shock |
      | Septal rupture | Acute ventricular septal defect with left-to-right shunt | New harsh holosystolic murmur and thrill |
      | Papillary muscle rupture | Acute severe mitral regurgitation | Flash pulmonary edema; murmur may be soft |

      Rupture occurs classically on days 3 to 7, at the time of maximal
      softening, but may occur earlier after late reperfusion. Its
      incidence was historically 1 to 5% and is now below 1% in the era of
      primary PCI, but mortality remains very high. Risk factors are age
      over 60, female sex, first infarction (no collaterals), anterior or
      lateral transmural infarct, absence of left ventricular hypertrophy,
      and late or no reperfusion.

      **Pericarditis, thrombus, and aneurysm**

      | Complication | Timing | Pathology | Clinical features |
      |---|---|---|---|
      | Early pericarditis | Days 1–3 | Fibrinous or fibrinohemorrhagic, over a transmural infarct | Pleuritic pain, friction rub; usually self-limited |
      | Mural thrombus | Days to weeks | Stasis over akinetic segments, endocardial injury, dilation | Systemic embolism, such as stroke; anticoagulate when present |
      | True aneurysm | Weeks to months | Wide-necked outpouching lined by thin scar | Heart failure, ventricular tachycardia, mural thrombus; rupture is rare |
      | Pseudoaneurysm | Days to weeks | Contained free wall rupture; narrow neck; wall of pericardium and thrombus | High risk of rupture; urgent surgery |
      | Dressler syndrome | About 2–8 weeks | Autoimmune pericarditis from antibodies to myocardial or pericardial antigens | Fever, pleuritic pain, effusion; now rare in the reperfusion era |

      **Late complications: remodeling and heart failure**

      Infarct expansion stretches and thins the necrotic segment and
      dilates the chamber. Non-infarcted myocardium undergoes compensatory
      hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and a more spherical shape. These
      changes are initially adaptive but ultimately raise wall stress and
      oxygen demand. Cumulative loss of contractile mass, remodeling, and
      ischemia of viable muscle produce ischemic cardiomyopathy. Therapies
      that limit remodeling in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction
      are ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin
      receptor-neprilysin inhibitors, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid
      receptor antagonists, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors.

      **Prognosis and prevention**

      | Aspect | Detail |
      |---|---|
      | In-hospital mortality | Below about 7% overall; higher for STEMI than NSTEMI |
      | First-year mortality | About 20%, mostly within the first 30 days |
      | Later mortality | About 1–2% per year thereafter |
      | Poor prognostic factors | Large, anterior, or transmural infarct; low LVEF; heart failure or shock; age over 70; female sex; diabetes; prior infarction; chronic kidney disease |
      | Primary prevention | Control of risk factors before a first event |
      | Secondary prevention | Smoking cessation, cardiac rehabilitation, lipid lowering to target, antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockade, ACE inhibition or angiotensin receptor blockade, blood pressure and glycemic control |

      Mortality figures vary widely between registries, eras, and STEMI and
      NSTEMI populations, and should be read as approximate.

      Can the pathogenesis, morphology, and clinical course of myocardial
      infarction be integrated into a single framework, and how does that
      framework apply to individual patients?
    text: >-
      Ischemic heart disease is an imbalance of oxygen supply and demand,
      and every therapy either restores supply or reduces demand. Stable
      and vulnerable plaques are different lesions: stable plaques cause
      predictable angina, whereas vulnerable plaques undergo rupture or
      erosion with thrombosis, often at a site that was not critically
      stenotic. Necrosis begins subendocardially at 20 to 30 minutes and
      advances epicardially over 6 to 12 hours, so timing determines
      salvage. Morphology dates the infarct and predicts its complications.
      Acute reperfusion salvages muscle but changes the histologic picture,
      whereas chronic revascularization targets hibernating myocardium
      behind a fixed stenosis.
